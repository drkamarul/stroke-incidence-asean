## 1. Narrative overview

Stroke is the second leading cause of death and one of the largest contributors to disability-adjusted life-years (DALYs) lost across the Association of Southeast Asian Nations (ASEAN). The WHO South-East Asia Region accounts for over 40% of global stroke mortality, and the GBD 2019 analysis confirmed that ASEAN low- and middle-income countries face age-standardised stroke mortality rates roughly 3.6 times higher than high-income neighbours in the same region (Wasay et al. 2023; GBD 2019 Stroke Collaborators 2021). Country-level incidence estimates remain heterogeneous due to mixed data sources — population-based registries (Singapore, Malaysia), hospital-based registries (Vietnam RES-Q, Philippines), nationally representative surveys (Indonesia Riskesdas), and modelled estimates (GBD).

Within ASEAN, Singapore stands out for its declining ischaemic stroke incidence and low case-fatality, reflecting universal hospital coverage and a mature national registry (Tan et al. 2020; Venketasubramanian et al. 2015). Malaysia has a stable but modestly rising incidence with substantial growth in young-adult ischaemic stroke (Aziz et al. 2015; Aziz et al. 2021). Thailand's TES study provides the most robust community-based prevalence and incidence figures for the lower-Mekong subregion (Suwanwela 2014; Hanchaiphiboolkul et al. 2011). Vietnam shows one of the highest regional incidence rates and a large hospital registry footprint (Le et al. 2016; Mai et al. 2025), while Indonesia and the Philippines rely largely on national surveys, GBD modelling, and selected sub-national cohorts (Venketasubramanian et al. 2022; Navarro et al. 2014; Navarro et al. 2021). Brunei, Cambodia, Laos and Myanmar remain critically under-represented in the peer-reviewed literature; published incidence figures for these four countries come almost exclusively from GBD modelling rather than primary data collection.

Across the region, hypertension is the dominant attributable risk factor, with diabetes, smoking and dyslipidaemia accruing rapidly as ASEAN urbanises. Ischaemic stroke now consistently outnumbers haemorrhagic stroke in registry data, but the haemorrhagic proportion remains higher than in Western cohorts (typically 18–30%). These contrasts justify a comparative analysis using harmonised denominators (per 100,000 person-years, age-standardised to WHO world population).

## 3. Key themes & cross-country contrasts

1. **Income-gradient effect.** Crude and age-standardised incidence broadly tracks ASEAN income groupings inversely: high-income Singapore reports the lowest declining trajectory (Tan et al. 2020), upper-middle-income Malaysia and Thailand show stable-to-rising trends (Aziz et al. 2021; Suwanwela 2014), while Vietnam, Indonesia and the Philippines exhibit higher absolute burdens (Mai et al. 2025; Riskesdas 2018; Navarro et al. 2021).

2. **Detection vs incidence.** Singapore's low rate partly reflects superior case-ascertainment elsewhere being incomplete — its registry uses claims + discharge + death linkage (Venketasubramanian et al. 2015). Indonesia's Riskesdas figures rely on self-report and are likely under-estimates; the GBD 2019 model places true Indonesian incidence substantially higher (Wasay et al. 2023).

3. **Subtype mix.** Ischaemic stroke dominates everywhere in ASEAN (typically 70–80%), but intracerebral haemorrhage retains a higher relative share (18–30%) than in Western cohorts. Vietnam in particular shows higher ICH proportions in older hospital-based series (Le et al. 2016).

4. **Young-adult rise.** Both Singapore (Tan et al. 2020) and Malaysia (Aziz et al. 2021) document growing incidence in <65y, driven by smoking and uncontrolled diabetes — a critical theme for KIM's manuscript.

5. **Sex differential.** Men consistently have higher incidence but women have higher 28-day case-fatality in Malaysia (Aziz et al. 2021); Singapore's incidence decline is also stronger in women.

6. **Surveillance gap.** Per Wasay et al. (2023), systematic stroke surveillance in WHO SEAR exists only in India and Thailand. Within ASEAN proper, Singapore and Malaysia have the most mature national registries; Vietnam runs the RES-Q hospital network; other countries rely on GBD modelling.
